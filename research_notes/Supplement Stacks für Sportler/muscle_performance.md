## Q1: Protein. Daily target (bulk vs cut), per-meal dose and distribution, pre-sleep protein, whey vs casein vs plant, leucine threshold, and what changed in 2023–2026

### Takeaway
On average, fat-free-mass gains from resistance training (RT) plateau at about 1.6 g protein/kg/day (Morton 2018 meta-analysis), so 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day is the practical bulking range. Lean lifters in a deficit need more: 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass (FFM), per Helms 2014 (see Q5).

Trommelen 2023 found that 100 g of protein gave a larger anabolic response lasting more than 12 h than 25 g did. That ends the idea of a hard "~20–40 g per meal" cap and makes the daily total the main lever. Spreading protein (≥4 × ~0.4 g/kg) and taking 20–40 g casein before sleep are secondary refinements. With adequate total intake, plant and animal protein give similar lean-mass and strength gains in younger adults (Nutrition Reviews meta-analysis 2025).

### Cited Findings
#### Daily target
- **Morton et al. 2018** (BJSM; systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of RCTs in healthy adults). Protein intakes above ~1.6 g/kg/day did not further increase RT-induced gains in FFM. — [Morton 2018 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/); [PubMed 28698222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)
- The RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day "appears insufficient for those who have the goal of gaining greater strength and fat-free mass with resistance exercise training." [check: this sentence came up in the same search, but its attribution to Morton 2018 is not certain] — [Morton 2018 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/)
- **Tagawa et al. 2022** (Sports Medicine – Open; dose-response meta-analysis of RCTs). With RT, strength gains rose 0.72 % (95 % CI 0.40–1.04 %) per +0.1 g/kg/day of total protein, up to 1.5 g/kg/day. No further gains were seen above that. — [Tagawa 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9441410/)
- **Nutrition Reviews 2021** dose-response meta-analysis (protein intake vs muscle-mass increase, RCTs). The search summary described a consistent lean-mass plateau around 1.5–1.6 g/kg/day. [check: exact coefficients not captured] — [Nutr Rev 2021](https://academic.oup.com/nutritionreviews/article/79/1/66/5936522); [medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.02.21.20026252.full.pdf)
- **Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018** (JISSN; narrative review) [older]:
  - Target 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day, split over at least 4 meals of 0.4 g/kg each.
  - At the 2.2 g/kg/day end that means up to 0.55 g/kg/meal.
  - Example: 75 kg → ~30 g × 4 = 120 g/day.

  — [Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29497353/); [full-text PDF](https://scholarlycommons.pacific.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1012&context=biochem)

#### Per-meal dose: the 2023 change
- **Trommelen et al. 2023** (Cell Reports Medicine 4(12):101324, 19 Dec 2023; human study with quadruple isotope tracer feeding-infusion):
  - 100 g protein after exercise gave a larger and longer (>12 h) anabolic response than 25 g.
  - The authors conclude that the magnitude and duration of the anabolic response "is not restricted and has previously been underestimated."

  — [Cell Rep Med 2023](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports-medicine/fulltext/S2666-3791(23)00540-2); [PMC10772463](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10772463/); [BarBend news summary](https://barbend.com/news/new-study-protein-timing-100-grams-not-wasted-muscle-growth/) [secondary]
- IJSNEM 34(5) published a commentary on the study in 2024. Its content was not captured. — [IJSNEM 2024 commentary](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/34/5/article-p322.xml)
- **The older "muscle-full" argument** for splitting protein: muscle protein synthesis rises for ~2–3 h after a protein meal, then falls even while amino acids are still elevated. [secondary] — [Scinergy summary](https://www.scinergy.io/learn/protein-per-meal-for-muscle-growth)
- A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis asks whether protein timing affects exercise-induced adaptations. Its conclusions were not captured. — [Protein-timing SR/MA 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12250900/)

#### Pre-sleep protein
- 20–40 g casein about 30 min before sleep raises whole-body protein synthesis overnight in young and older men (narrative review). — [Physical Activity and Nutrition review](https://www.e-pan.org/journal/view.php?number=232)
- **Systematic review, J Sci Med Sport 2021:**
  - Pre-sleep protein can increase muscle-fibre cross-sectional area, strength and muscle mass over 10–12 weeks of RT in young men, but not in elderly men.
  - The conclusions "need to be taken with caution due to uneven protein intakes between experimental groups." The benefit may therefore come from more total daily protein rather than timing.

  — [JSAMS systematic review](https://www.jsams.org/article/S1440-2440(20)30719-2/abstract)
- **Joy et al. 2018** (JISSN; preliminary RCT) [older]. Daytime and night-time casein increased muscle size and strength similarly. This supports "achieving specific daily protein levels versus specific timing." — [Joy 2018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/s12970-018-0228-9)

#### Protein source (whey, casein, plant)
- **Nutrition Reviews 2025** (systematic review and meta-analysis, 43 RCTs):
  - Protein source did not affect changes in absolute lean mass or muscle strength.
  - Animal protein was favoured for *percent* lean mass.
  - Animal protein was significantly better than plant protein in older adults, but not in younger adults.

  — [Nutr Rev 2025 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12166177/); [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/nutritionreviews/article/83/7/e1581/7954494)
- **2025 RCT (JISSN).** An animal-based and a plant-based protein blend, each given as extra dietary protein, produced similar muscle adaptations to RT. — [JISSN 2025 RCT](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15502783.2025.2568047); [PMC12509290](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12509290/)
- **Also exist, results not captured:**
  - A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis (Frontiers in Nutrition) on long-term plant vs animal protein supplementation: body composition, strength, performance, cardiometabolic markers.
  - A SportRxiv preprint meta-analysis on muscle protein synthesis.

  — [Frontiers 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2026.1813846/full); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/526)

### Inferences
- **Bulk:** 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day. At 80 kg that is ~130–175 g/day (arithmetic). Going much above ~2.2 g/kg/day is unlikely to add muscle. It mainly costs money and takes calories away from carbohydrate and fat.
- **Cut:** move to the higher range. Helms 2014 gives 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM (see Q5). At 80 kg and ~15 % body fat, FFM is 68 kg, which gives ~155–210 g/day.
- **Distribution:** at least 4 protein meals of at least 0.4 g/kg (~30–40 g) is still a sound default. Since Trommelen 2023, though, larger or fewer meals are not "wasted." Fitting protein around the daily schedule is legitimate as long as the daily total is reached.
- **Pre-sleep:** 20–40 g of slow protein is an easy extra feeding, e.g., casein powder or casein-rich dairy such as German Magerquark. Its proven benefit probably works through the higher daily total.
- **Whey vs casein vs plant:** for a young trainee who meets the total, choose by price, tolerance and taste. Plant-only eaters can use blends and slightly larger servings. That compensation strategy is not tested in the captured evidence.
- **Powder is food, not an ergogenic.** Protein powder adds value only when it closes a gap to the daily target.

### Gaps
- **Morton 2018 exact breakpoint:** often cited as 1.62 g/kg/day, with an upper 95 % CI of ~2.2 g/kg/day. Not captured verbatim in this session.
- **Leucine threshold:** no verified per-meal number was captured. The commonly cited ~2–3 g leucine/meal is unverified here.
- **Uncaptured conclusions:** the 2025 protein-timing meta-analysis and the 2026 Frontiers plant-vs-animal meta-analysis.
- **Whey vs casein:** no 2024–2026 head-to-head data on chronic hypertrophy in trained young adults was captured.
- **Not researched (search budget exhausted):** safety of high protein intake (e.g., kidney questions in healthy people) and German DGE reference values.
- **Cost:** per-kg cost of whey, casein and plant protein in Germany was not collected.

## Q2: Creatine monohydrate. Dose, loading, timing, effect sizes, safety (kidneys, hair/DHT, water), forms, and 2024–2026 evidence

### Takeaway
Creatine monohydrate is the best-supported supplement for this goal. The IOC 2018 consensus rates its evidence "adequate," and the AIS lists it in Group A.
- **Effects:** meta-analyses from 2022–2025 show about +1.1 kg extra lean mass with RT. In adults under 50 they show clear strength gains vs placebo: ~+4.4 kg upper body and ~+11.4 kg lower body. One analysis found a sex difference, with significant gains in men and none in women.
- **Safety:** new data is reassuring. A 2025 RCT found no change in DHT, the DHT:testosterone ratio or hair measures. A 2026 meta-analysis of 26 RCTs found no kidney harm.
- **Form:** use powder. In one lab test, 4 of 6 gummies failed their label claim while all powders passed. HCl has no demonstrated advantage.

### Cited Findings
#### Effect sizes (meta-analyses 2022–2026)
- **Wang et al. 2024** (Nutrients; adults <50 y, systematic review and meta-analysis):
  - Creatine plus RT increased upper-body strength by 4.43 kg and lower-body strength by 11.35 kg vs placebo.
  - The search summary also reported significant gains in males and non-significant gains in females. [check: the sex subgroup may come from the related 2025 upper/lower-body strength-and-power meta-analysis]

  — [Wang 2024 (PMC11547435)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11547435/); [2025 SR/MA on upper/lower-body strength and power (PMC12430374)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12430374/)
- **Delpino et al. 2022** (Nutrition; systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs). Creatine plus RT increased lean body mass by ~1.1 kg vs control, regardless of age. — [Delpino 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0899900722002040)
- **Older adults:** creatine plus strength training for 7–52 weeks increased lean tissue mass by ~1.37 kg. [check: the number came via the search summary; source is either the European Review of Aging and Physical Activity 2025 meta-analysis or the 2025 older-adults meta-analysis] — [ERAPA 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s11556-025-00392-9); [PMC12506341](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12506341/)
- **Also exist, results not captured:**
  - A 2025 JISSN systematic review with dose-response meta-analysis comparing novice and experienced lifters (studies up to March 2025).
  - A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in young men comparing RT and non-RT contexts.

  — [JISSN 2025 novice vs experienced](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15502783.2025.2586523); [PMC13099317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13099317/)

#### Dose used in current evidence
- The 2025 hair/DHT RCT used 5 g/day creatine monohydrate vs 5 g/day maltodextrin for 12 weeks. — [Lak et al. 2025, JISSN](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15502783.2025.2495229)
- 3–5 g/day, "recommended doses," described as safe for kidney function in healthy adults, per 2026 meta-analyses and cohort studies. [secondary] — [Ubie Doctor's Note 2026](https://ubiehealth.com/doctors-note/creatine-kidneys-function-safe-study-facts-2026-2751q2)

#### Safety
- **Hair/DHT: 2025 RCT (JISSN).**
  - 45 resistance-trained men, aged 18–40, took 5 g/day for 12 weeks.
  - DHT, the DHT-to-testosterone ratio and hair-growth parameters did not differ from placebo.
  - It was the first study to assess hair-follicle health directly after creatine.

  — [Lak 2025 (Taylor & Francis)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15502783.2025.2495229); [PubMed 40265319](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40265319/)
- **Kidneys: 2026 meta-analysis** (International Urology and Nephrology; 26 RCTs).
  - Creatine did not harm kidney function in adults, including people with pre-existing CKD on haemodialysis.
  - It is described as the first meta-analysis to split GFR results by measurement method (creatinine-based vs Cr-EDTA). This separates measurement artefacts from true kidney injury.

  — [Int Urol Nephrol 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11255-026-05287-x); [PubMed 42507286](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42507286/)
- **Serum creatinine:** a modest rise after starting creatine "typically reflects increased creatine turnover — not kidney damage." Individual factors still matter: pre-existing kidney conditions, hydration, dose and how lab results are interpreted. [secondary] — [Ubie 2026](https://ubiehealth.com/doctors-note/creatine-kidneys-function-safe-study-facts-2026-2751q2); related: [Short review of common creatine safety concerns (PMC12702719)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12702719/)

#### Forms and product quality
- **Gummies: SuppCo lab test** [secondary].
  - SuppCo bought 11 products on Amazon (5 powders, 6 gummies) and had an ISO-accredited lab test them.
  - 4 of the 6 gummies failed. One best-seller labelled 5 g/serving contained 0.005 g. Another contained 0.025 g. Two had no detectable creatine.
  - All 5 powders came in at 98–101 % of their label claim.
  - Explanation offered: in warm, moist gummy manufacture and on the shelf, creatine converts to inactive creatinine.

  — [SuppCo tested](https://supp.co/articles/suppco-tested-creatine-testing-results-gummies-failed-lab-analysis)
- NOW Foods reported "widespread failings" in its own gummy tests. Media coverage continued into Feb 2026. [secondary] — [Nutraceuticals World](https://www.nutraceuticalsworld.com/breaking-news/now-reports-widespread-failings-in-creatine-gummy-tests/); [National Today, 22 Feb 2026](https://nationaltoday.com/us/ny/new-york/news/2026/02/22/creatine-gummies-tested-some-brands-barely-contain-creatine/); [Arnold's Pump Club](https://arnoldspumpclub.com/blogs/newsletter/the-creatine-gummy-scandal)
- **Creatine HCl.**
  - Claim: 1.65 relative bioavailability vs monohydrate ("~50 % higher") under FDA-standard methods. [check: the claim appears to trace to HCl patent or manufacturer literature]
  - A review describes HCl as more soluble, and found that both forms improved performance and maximal strength.

  — [US patent "Creatine oral supplementation using creatine hydrochloride salt"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8962685); [review: CrHCl vs CrM](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/372688550_Creatine_Hydrochloride_Versus_Creatine_MonohydrateDifferences_in_Solubility_Ergogenic_Effects_and_Body_Composition)
- **2025 low-dose monohydrate vs HCl RCT.** Triple-blind, placebo-controlled, in elite team-sport athletes. Its outcome could NOT be reliably captured: the snippet echoed my query wording. — [PMC12291177](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12291177/); [NCT05697900](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05697900)
- An RCT compared creatine nitrate (± creatinine) with monohydrate. Its results were not captured. — [PMC6848817](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6848817/)

#### Position statements
- The IOC 2018 consensus lists creatine (monohydrate) among the supplements with adequate evidence for marginal performance gains. AIS lists creatine in Group A (see Q6). — [IOC 2018 PDF](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/2018_dietary-supplements-high-performance-athlete.pdf); [AIS framework summary](https://www.dietitianapproved.com/blog/aissupplementframework) [secondary]

### Inferences
- **Stack core:** 3–5 g/day creatine monohydrate powder, daily, in both bulk and cut. This matches the dose used in the 2025 RCT and the dose range the 2026 sources call kidney-safe.
- **Timing:** no timing data was captured. Trials used once-daily dosing over weeks, so consistency matters more than clock time (inference).
- **What to expect:** roughly +1 kg lean mass. Part of it may be water, but no figure was captured to quantify that. Expect a better strength trajectory too. For women, the strength effect looks less certain in one analysis.
- **Tell your doctor before blood tests.** Creatinine-based eGFR can falsely suggest reduced kidney function, which is why the 2026 meta-analysis split results by measurement method. Anyone with known kidney disease should use creatine only under medical supervision.
- **Buy plain powder.** Avoid gummies and "advanced" salts; plain monohydrate powder is the most reliable form. For doping-risk-minimised products in Germany, see the Kölner Liste (Q6).

### Gaps
- **Loading protocol:** commonly cited as ~20 g/day, or 0.3 g/kg/day in 4 doses, for 5–7 days, then 3–5 g/day (ISSN 2017). Not verified in this session. Neither the ISSN 2017 creatine position stand nor the 2021 "common questions and misconceptions" paper was captured.
- **Water retention and timing:** no figure on the size of water retention, and no pre- vs post-workout timing data, was captured.
- **Unverified results:** the 2025 novice-vs-experienced dose-response meta-analysis, the 2026 young-men meta-analysis, and the 2025 monohydrate-vs-HCl RCT.
- **Origin of the DHT concern:** the original 2009 study in rugby players was not retrieved.
- **Cost:** cost per daily dose in Germany was not collected.

## Q3: Caffeine, beta-alanine, citrulline, nitrate, bicarbonate, glycerol, HMB and ketone esters. What helps resistance training specifically, with effect sizes and doses

### Takeaway
For resistance training, the picture by supplement:
- **Caffeine:** the only acute aid with consistent RT benefits, and the benefits are small. The 1RM effect size is ~0.17–0.20; bar velocity (SMD 0.42) and power improve more. ISSN recommends 3–6 mg/kg about 60 min before training. But EFSA's single-dose "no concern" level is 200 mg (~3 mg/kg at 70 kg), and a pre-workout dose costs sleep unless taken ~13 h before bed.
- **Nitrate:** improves reps-to-failure (SMD 0.43) and mean velocity (SMD 0.57) in squat and bench.
- **Bicarbonate:** improves muscular endurance (d = 0.37) but not strength (−0.03).
- **Beta-alanine:** small (ES 0.18), mostly for 1–10 min efforts, with no body-composition effect.
- **Citrulline malate:** trivial to very small (g = 0.16, GRADE low to very low).
- **HMB:** no benefit in trained young adults (meta-analysis 2020). The flagship HMB-FA study is contested.
- **Ketone esters and glycerol:** no resistance-training use case.

### Cited Findings
#### Caffeine
- **ISSN position stand 2021** (Guest et al.):
  - Dose: 3–6 mg/kg; 60 min before exercise is the most common timing.
  - Effects on muscular endurance, movement velocity, muscular strength, sprinting, jumping and throwing are "small to moderate."
  - Aerobic endurance shows the most consistent moderate-to-large benefit, and individual responses differ.

  — [ISSN 2021 caffeine](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4); [NutraIngredients report](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2021/01/07/ISSN-confirms-caffeine-position-as-a-sporting-performance-aid/) [secondary]
- **1RM strength:** in pooled meta-analyses, 1–7 mg/kg improves 1RM slightly (ES 0.17–0.20, trivial to small). — [2025 SR/MA on muscular power (PMC12537405)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12537405/)
- **Grgic et al. meta-analysis** [older, check]:
  - Caffeine increased upper-body 1RM, raw difference ~3.5 kg.
  - It did not increase lower-body 1RM: ~1.7 kg, with a 95 % CI of −1.7 to 5.0 kg.
  - The upper-body CI in the snippet (1.5–4.8 kg) looks garbled.

  — [Grgic 2018, JISSN](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/s12970-018-0216-0); [Grgic et al. 2018 review (VU repository)](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/37418/1/Epr37418Manuscript-Grgic_et_al.(2018)TheInfluenceof_CaffeineSupplementation_on_Resistance_Exercise_A_%20Review_.pdf)
- **2025 meta-analysis on power during resistance exercise:**
  - Mean velocity SMD 0.42 (95 % CI 0.19–0.65); mean power SMD 0.21 (0.12–0.30).
  - The velocity gain was larger in males (SMD 0.56 vs 0.22).
  - It was also larger in habitual consumers under 3 mg/kg/day (0.87 vs 0.21), which suggests partial tolerance.

  — [PMC12537405](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12537405/)
- A 2024 umbrella meta-analysis (Heliyon) on caffeine, muscular strength and endurance exists. Its figures were not captured. — [Heliyon 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11336343/)
- **EFSA safety opinion 2015** (healthy, non-pregnant adults):
  - Habitual intake up to 400 mg/day raises no safety concerns.
  - Single doses up to 200 mg (~3 mg/kg for a 70 kg adult) raise no safety concerns.
  - The same 200 mg limit holds within 2 h before intense exercise under normal environmental conditions.

  — [EFSA Journal 2015;13(5):4102](https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/4102); [EUFIC summary](https://www.eufic.org/en/whats-in-food/article/efsa-opinion-on-the-safety-of-caffeine)
- **Sleep: Gardiner et al. 2023** (Sleep Medicine Reviews; systematic review and meta-analysis):
  - Caffeine cut total sleep time by 45 min and sleep efficiency by 7 %.
  - It lengthened sleep-onset latency by 9 min and wake after sleep onset by 12 min.
  - Lost sleep time shrank by 2.8 min for every extra hour between caffeine and bedtime, and grew by 0.2 min per extra mg.
  - Cut-offs: coffee (107 mg/250 mL) at least 8.8 h before bed; a standard pre-workout serving (217.5 mg) at least 13.2 h before bed.

  — [Gardiner 2023 (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1087079223000205); [Leeds Beckett repository](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/9625/)
- A 2025 Sleep Medicine meta-analysis on age- and dose-specific effects of caffeine on sleep exists. Its results were not captured. — [Sleep Med 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1389945725005490)
- **Interaction:** the German BfR warns that caffeine combined with synephrine may be unsafe because the cardiovascular effects add up (see Q4). — [BfR opinion](https://www.bfr.bund.de/en/opinions/health-assessment-of-sports-and-weight-loss-products-containing-synephrine-and-caffeine/)

#### Beta-alanine
- **ISSN position stand 2015** (Trexler et al.) [older]:
  - 4 weeks at 4–6 g/day significantly increases muscle carnosine.
  - The only reported side effect is paraesthesia (tingling).
  - Tingling is reduced by smaller split doses (1.6 g) or a sustained-release formula.

  — [ISSN 2015 beta-alanine (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4501114/); [PubMed 26175657](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26175657/)
- **Saunders et al. 2017 meta-analysis** (40 studies, n = 1,461) [older]:
  - Overall ES 0.18, which is 2.1 times smaller than the 2012 meta-analysis.
  - Exercise capacity (time to exhaustion) ES ~0.50; performance (time trials) ES ~0.11.
  - Efforts of ~1–10 min benefited most.

  — [GSSI Sports Science Exchange summary](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/an-update-on-beta-alanine-supplementation-for-athletes) [secondary]; [Saunders 2017 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/310617184_b-alanine_supplementation_to_improve_exercise_capacity_and_performance_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis)
- **2022 GRADE-assessed meta-analysis** (JISSN). No significant body-composition change in any subgroup (dose, duration, exercise type). Its "capacity to boost hypertrophic responses during resistance training remains unknown." — [JISSN 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15502783.2022.2079384); [PMC9261744](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9261744/)
- **2025 JISSN systematic review** of dosing strategies for strength and power:
  - 4–6.4 g/day, especially split into ~0.8 g doses several times a day, was more likely to improve maximal strength and power outcomes.
  - It works mainly when muscle acidity is high: repeated submaximal efforts, short rests, high-volume hypertrophy-style training.
  - It may not raise maximal strength directly, but it improves fatigue resistance, which can support more training volume.
  - [check: a statement that the beta-alanine group did more reps in bench press and squat could not be pinned to a specific study]

  — [JISSN 2025 dosing review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15502783.2025.2566368); [PMC12466178](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12466178/)
- **Also exist, results not captured:**
  - A 2024 IJSNEM meta-analysis on maximal-intensity exercise in trained young men.
  - An RCT titled "Does beta-alanine supplementation enhance adaptations to resistance training?"
  - A 2019 systematic risk assessment of oral beta-alanine.

  — [IJSNEM 2024](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/34/6/article-p397.xml); [RCT (PMC9806742)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9806742/); [risk assessment (PubMed 30980076)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30980076/)

#### L-citrulline / citrulline malate (CM)
- **Vårvik et al. 2021** (IJSNEM; meta-analysis of 8 double-blind trials, n = 137). 8 g CM taken 40–60 min before training added about 3 repetitions to failure (+6.4 %). [secondary: numbers taken from a summary site] — [IJSNEM 2021](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/31/4/article-p350.xml); [FitCraft summary](https://getfitcraft.com/science/citrulline-malate-exercise-research) [secondary]
- **2026 three-level meta-analysis** (Nutrients 18(12):1881; 30 trials, 138 effect sizes, n = 644):
  - The overall effect was small (Hedges' g = 0.16), with GRADE certainty low to very low.
  - Muscular endurance showed a "very small, marginal" effect that held after outliers were removed.
  - Strength and power effects were trivial.

  — [Nutrients 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/18/12/1881); [PMC13304508](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13304508/)
- **Recovery:** a 2017 study found CM did not improve muscle recovery after resistance exercise in untrained young men (title-level finding). — [Nutrients 2017 (PMC5691748)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5691748/)
- **Also exist, results not captured:** a 2025 JISSN RCT (6 weeks, L-citrulline vs L-citrulline DL-malate in resistance-trained men) and a 2023 crossover on caffeine plus CM. — [JISSN 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15502783.2025.2513944); [PMC10468939](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10468939/)

#### Dietary nitrate (beetroot juice)
- **2023 meta-analysis on back squat and bench press** (Nutrients 15(11):2493; literature to April 2023):
  - Nitrate improved repetitions to failure (SMD 0.43), mean power (SMD 0.40) and mean velocity (SMD 0.57).
  - It did not improve peak power or peak velocity.

  — [Nutrients 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/15/11/2493); [PMC10255616](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10255616/)
- **2023 meta-analysis of single and repeated short high-intensity bouts** (Antioxidants 12(6):1194; 27 studies; literature to Jan 2023). Improved: time to peak power (SMD 0.75), mean power (0.20) and Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test distance (0.17). — [Antioxidants 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3921/12/6/1194)
- **2024 meta-analysis on cycling sprints.** Nitrate improved time to peak power, but not mean, peak or minimum power. — [PMC11357493](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11357493/)
- **Also exist, results not captured:**
  - A 2025 umbrella review of 20 meta-analyses (Sports Medicine).
  - A 2025 dose-response study on resistance-exercise performance.
  - A 2025 meta-analysis in females.

  — [Sports Med 2025 umbrella review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02194-6); [PubMed 40274664](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40274664/); [Females meta-analysis (PMC12471447)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12471447/)

#### Sodium bicarbonate
- **ISSN position stand 2021** (Grgic et al.). 0.2–0.5 g/kg improves muscular-endurance performance. The benefit is established mainly for high-intensity tasks lasting 30 s to 12 min. — [ISSN 2021 bicarbonate](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/s12970-021-00458-w); [PMC8427947](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8427947/)
- **Grgic et al. 2020** (Sports Medicine; systematic review and meta-analysis). Muscular endurance improved (12 studies; Cohen's d = 0.37, 95 % CI 0.15–0.59). Muscular strength did not (11 studies; pooled ES −0.03). — [Grgic 2020, Sports Med](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-020-01275-y); [VU repository PDF](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/40625/1/Effects_%20of_sodium_bicarbonate_supplementation_on_muscular_strength_and_endurance_a_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis.pdf)
- In one of these analyses, 19 of 20 included studies used 0.3 g/kg and one used 0.4 g/kg. [check: which analysis is not clear from the snippet] — [ISSN 2021 bicarbonate](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/s12970-021-00458-w)
- **Also exist, results not captured:** a 2021 JISSN umbrella review, and a study of caffeine plus bicarbonate for muscular endurance. — [JISSN umbrella review 2021](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/s12970-021-00469-7); [PMC11677328](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11677328/)

#### Glycerol
- **WADA status:** glycerol went onto WADA's list in 2010 as a masking agent (S5), because plasma-volume expansion could alter blood parameters. It was removed on 1 Jan 2018 because the effects on plasma volume and Athlete Biological Passport parameters were judged minimal. It is now permitted. — [USADA 2018 list summary](https://www.usada.org/athlete-advisory/2018-prohibited-list-summary-of-major-changes/); [UKAD 2018](https://www.ukad.org.uk/news/article/wada-updates-the-prohibited-list-for-2018)
- **AIS status:** AIS files glycerol (for hyperhydration) under Group A performance supplements. — [AIS glycerol page (URL path /group_a/)](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/glycerol/glycerol-is-a-permitted-substance); [AIS glycerol fact sheet (copy)](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Sport-supplement-fact-sheets-Glycerol-v4.pdf)

#### HMB (incl. the HMB free-acid controversy)
- **Wilson et al. 2014** (Eur J Appl Physiol; 12-week RCT of HMB free acid in resistance-trained people). Reported a ~7.4 kg lean-mass gain and large strength gains vs placebo, effect sizes "unprecedented for any legal supplement." [secondary] — [PubMed 24599749](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24599749/); [mysportscience (Jeukendrup)](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/does_hmb_work) [secondary]; [Legion research review](https://legionathletics.com/hmb-supplement/) [secondary]
- **The dispute:**
  - Gentles & Phillips (2017) raised "discrepancies in publications related to HMB-FA and ATP supplementation," for example asking where numbers in Table 1 came from.
  - The original authors published a response in Nutrition & Metabolism (2017).
  - Independent replications have generally not reproduced effects of this size, and many positive HMB studies come from groups with potential conflicts of interest. [secondary]

  — [Response to discrepancies (Nutr Metab 2017)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12986-017-0227-x); [PMC5704583](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5704583/)
- **Jakubowski et al. 2020** (Nutrients; systematic review and meta-analysis). HMB "does not improve resistance exercise-induced changes in body composition or strength in young subjects." — [Jakubowski 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7285233/)
- **Bioavailability, 2023** (Amino Acids). The calcium salt had *superior* bioavailability compared with the free-acid form. This undercuts the premium marketing of HMB-FA. — [Amino Acids 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00726-023-03369-z)
- **AIS status:** Group C. — [AIS Group C](https://www.ais.gov.au/nutrition/supplements/group_c)

#### Ketone esters
- **Vandoorne et al. 2017** (Frontiers in Physiology) [older]. A ketone-ester drink during recovery increased mTORC1 signalling (S6K1 and 4E-BP1 phosphorylation). It did not improve muscle glycogen resynthesis. — [Front Physiol 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.00310/full); [PMC5440563](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5440563/)
- **Muscle damage (2020).** A ketone monoester did not speed recovery from eccentric exercise. Maximal voluntary contraction at 24 h and 48 h and jump height did not differ from control. — [PMC7752861](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7752861/)
- **Mechanism only so far.** In cultured muscle cells, leucine plus ketone bodies roughly doubled muscle protein synthesis vs leucine alone. Whether chronic ketone-ester intake improves training adaptation "needs to be further investigated." — [Frontiers 2020 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2020.613648/full); [AJP-Cell review](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/ajpcell.00485.2023); [Adv Nutr perspective "Does it work?"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2161831322001855)
- A registered trial is testing exogenous ketosis and muscle protein synthesis during recovery. — [NCT06769100](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06769100)

### Inferences
- **Ranking for a hypertrophy trainee** (full table in Q6):
  - Caffeine: worth using acutely, at modest doses, for key sessions.
  - Nitrate and beta-alanine: optional, low-priority extras for high-rep, high-volume blocks.
  - Bicarbonate: works, but suits competition better than 5×/week gym use. GI tolerance was not captured, and the sodium load is large.
  - Citrulline, HMB, ketone esters and glycerol: not worth buying for this goal.
- **Acute boosts ≠ proven hypertrophy.** For caffeine, nitrate, citrulline and bicarbonate, the evidence covers performance within a session. No captured chronic trial shows the extra reps turn into more hypertrophy. Beta-alanine showed no body-composition effect. Only creatine and protein have direct lean-mass evidence.
- **Caffeine within EFSA limits:**
  - At 80 kg, EFSA's 200 mg single dose equals ~2.5 mg/kg.
  - ISSN's 3–6 mg/kg would be 240–480 mg, which exceeds EFSA's "no concern" single dose.
  - The meta-analytic 1RM effects come from 1–7 mg/kg, so ~2–3 mg/kg (~150–200 mg) plausibly captures much of the benefit (inference).
- **Evening training and sleep (Gardiner model, 23:00 bedtime):** a 217.5 mg pre-workout would have to be taken before ~09:50, and a 107 mg coffee before ~14:10 (arithmetic). For after-work training, a low dose or caffeine-free sessions protect sleep, which recovery depends on (inference).
- **Tolerance:** effects are larger in low habitual users. That argues for moderate daily caffeine, or saving pre-workout caffeine for heavy days (inference).
- **Beta-alanine, if used:** 3.2–6.4 g/day in 0.8–1.6 g doses, daily for at least 4 weeks. Timing doesn't matter because it builds up muscle carnosine over weeks (inference). The tingling is the only reported side effect (ISSN).
- **Bicarbonate sodium load:** 0.3 g/kg × 80 kg = 24 g sodium bicarbonate ≈ 6.6 g sodium (27 % sodium by mass; arithmetic). That matters for blood pressure and GI tolerance.

### Gaps
- **Nitrate:** dose (often cited as ~6–13 mmol ≈ 400–800 mg nitrate, 2–3 h before exercise), the effect of antibacterial mouthwash, and safety were not captured.
- **Bicarbonate:** rates of GI side effects and ways to reduce them (enteric capsules, split doses, taking with a meal) were not captured.
- **Glycerol:** dose and side effects were not captured, and no RT data was found.
- **HMB:** dose (usually 3 g/day) and the details of the ISSN 2013 position stand were not captured.
- **Ketone esters:** AIS classification was not captured.
- **Caffeine:** ISSN's minimal effective dose, CYP1A2 genetics and EFSA's pregnancy intake level were not captured.
- **Uncaptured results:** the 2025 nitrate umbrella review, the 2024 IJSNEM beta-alanine meta-analysis, the beta-alanine RT RCT, and the 2025 citrulline vs citrulline-malate RCT.
- **Cost:** cost per dose was not collected.

## Q4: What does not work or is risky (BCAAs/EAAs, glutamine, "testosterone boosters", ecdysterone/turkesterone, SARMs/prohormones, fat burners, high-dose antioxidants)

### Takeaway
None of these belong in a rational stack.
- **BCAAs/EAAs and glutamine:** add nothing for hypertrophy or strength when protein is at least 1.6 g/kg/day.
- **"Testosterone boosters":** mostly untested; one analysis found no studies for 67 of 109 ingredients. D-aspartic acid at 6 g/day even *lowered* testosterone. AIS puts the whole category in Group D because of contamination and doping risk.
- **Turkesterone:** failed two small RCTs (2024, 2025).
- **Ecdysterone:** not banned, but on WADA's 2026 Monitoring Program.
- **SARMs:** unapproved drugs, banned by WADA and criminally relevant in Germany under the AntiDopG. Linked to drug-induced liver injury, suppression of the body's own testosterone, and lower HDL.
- **Stimulant fat burners:** the BfR flags synephrine plus caffeine as potentially unsafe, and yohimbe bark is prohibited in EU foods.
- **High-dose vitamins C/E:** may blunt RT adaptations in young trained people; the evidence is mixed.

### Cited Findings
#### BCAAs / EAAs
- **Plotkin et al. 2021** (IJSNEM; narrative review of isolated leucine and BCAA supplementation):
  - Adding a BCAA supplement to adequate protein from other sources gave no extra hypertrophy.
  - Current evidence does not support BCAA or leucine supplements for hypertrophy in people who already meet "≥1.6 g/kg/day."
  - [check: which of the two 2025 reviews carries the ≥1.6 g/kg sentence]

  — [2025 review "Nutritional Supplements for Muscle Hypertrophy" (PMC12655760)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12655760/); [2025 narrative review on amino-acid supplementation (PMC12530991)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12530991/)
- **2021 systematic review of human RCTs** (Science & Sports). Evidence for isolated BCAAs for hypertrophy is "equivocal." Recommendation: eat enough high-quality protein, with "no reason to consume BCAA supplements" to boost muscle protein synthesis. — [Science & Sports 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0765159721002008)

#### Glutamine
- **Candow et al. 2001** (Eur J Appl Physiol; RCT, n = 31, aged 18–24) [older]:
  - Dose: 0.9 g/kg lean tissue mass/day during 6 weeks of RT.
  - No significant effect on muscle performance, body composition or muscle protein breakdown.
  - Both groups gained ~30 % squat 1RM and ~14 % bench 1RM.

  — [Candow 2001](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-001-0523-y); [PubMed 11822473](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11822473/)
- A 2019 meta-analysis (Clinical Nutrition) on glutamine, athletic performance, body composition and immune function exists. Its results were not captured. — [Clin Nutr 2019](https://www.clinicalnutritionjournal.com/article/S0261-5614(18)30173-0/abstract)

#### "Testosterone boosters"
- **The Aging Male 2024** ("Navigating the unregulated terrain of testosterone boosters") [check: the findings are attributed to this paper based on how search results were grouped]:
  - Most common ingredients: zinc, fenugreek, vitamin B6, *Tribulus terrestris*, magnesium, boron, DIM, *Eurycoma longifolia* (tongkat ali) and maca.
  - 67 of 109 substances had no studies on their effects. 19 (17.4 %) had only one study on testosterone.
  - Tongkat ali, ashwagandha and fenugreek had the "strongest" evidence.

  — [Aging Male 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/13685538.2024.2312176)
- **Endocrines 2021 review** ("Testosterone Boosters Intake in Athletes") [check: attribution]:
  - Tribulus has no effect on testosterone in healthy men.
  - L-arginine, L-carnitine, *Serenoa repens*, selenium and boron do not appear to raise testosterone.

  — [Endocrines 2021 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/351624734_Testosterone_Boosters_Intake_in_Athletes_Current_Evidence_and_Further_Directions); [PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9b60/2aa310f5b7c11fde9131274ab820df6d1736.pdf)
- **Tongkat ali.** Trials of standardized extract at ~200–400 mg/day reported total-testosterone increases of ~15–25 %, largest in men who started low. [secondary, commercial site; not checked against the primary meta-analysis] — [Mito Health guide](https://mitohealth.com/guide/which-testosterone-booster)
- **D-aspartic acid: Melville et al. 2015** (JISSN; resistance-trained men). 6 g/day significantly *reduced* total testosterone (~12.5 % from baseline) and free testosterone (vs control). 3 g/day did not differ from control. — [Melville 2015](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/s12970-015-0078-7); [PMC4384294](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4384294/)
- **D-aspartic acid: Melville et al. 2017.** A 3-month RCT (PLOS One) exists. Its outcomes were not captured. — [PLOS One 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0182630)
- **AIS Group D** ("banned or at high risk of contamination") includes:
  - *Tribulus terrestris* and other testosterone boosters, and maca root powder.
  - DHEA, androstenedione, 19-norandrostenedione/-ol and other prohormones.

  — [AIS Group D](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_d)

#### Ecdysterone / turkesterone
- **WADA 2026 status of ecdysterone:**
  - Not prohibited.
  - Listed on the 2026 Monitoring Program as an anabolic agent, monitored in and out of competition.
  - The Monitoring Program carries no sanctions, but its data inform future List decisions.
  - The 2026 List and Monitoring Program have been in force since 1 Jan 2026.

  — [WADA 2026 Monitoring Program (PDF)](https://www.wada-ama.org/sites/default/files/2025-09/2026_list_monitoring_program_en_final_clean_september_2025.pdf); [WADA: 2026 List in force](https://www.wada-ama.org/en/news/wadas-2026-prohibited-list-now-force); [USADA on ecdysteroids](https://www.usada.org/dietary-supplements/athletes-need-know-ecdysteroids/)
- **2024 turkesterone RCT** ("A Preliminary Investigation of Turkesterone: It's Not Deca"):
  - Active men and women took 500 mg/day or placebo for 4 weeks.
  - DXA showed no significant differences in body mass, lean mass, fat mass or body-fat %.
  - [secondary: design details via a summary site]

  — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/383592776_A_Preliminary_Investigation_of_Turkesterone_It's_Not_Deca_Direct_Original_Research); [NSU symposium 2025 abstract](https://nsuworks.nova.edu/student_symposium/2025/program/80/)
- **2025 randomized double-blind trial** of a turkesterone-containing ecdysteroid product, 4 weeks. No differences vs placebo in body composition, handgrip strength, mood or sleep. [secondary: matched to this paper by title] — [PMC12379694](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12379694/); [Curex summary](https://getcurex.com/glp1-blog/is-turkesterone-good-nutrition-benefits) [secondary]

#### SARMs / prohormones
- **Germany: legal situation** [secondary, law-firm pages; must be checked against the statute]:
  - Most SARMs have no pharmaceutical approval in Germany, and nearly all are on the WADA Prohibited List.
  - Under the Anti-Doping Act (AntiDopG) they count as doping agents.
  - The Doping Agents Quantity Ordinance (DmMV) sets one "non-small quantity" (*nicht geringe Menge*) threshold for the whole SARM group: 540 mg of the free compound. What counts is the pure active substance, not the product's total weight.
  - Group orders and full-course orders, common in strength sports, can quickly exceed this threshold.
  - Athletes in competition are punishable for trading, possessing or using SARMs in any case.
  - Penalties: fines or up to 3 years in prison, and in special cases up to 10 years.
  - **[check]** One snippet says possession "in small quantities" is punishable for everyone. That contradicts the threshold logic for recreational (non-competing) users, so AntiDopG §§ 2–4 and the DmMV text need to be checked.

  — [Kanzlei Kämpf: SARMs & AntiDopG](https://www.kanzlei-kaempf.net/sarms-antidopg-strafe-verteidigung-fachanwalt/); [die-anwalts-kanzlei: SARMs – AntiDopG oder AMG](https://www.die-anwalts-kanzlei.de/arzneimittelstrafrecht/sarms-antidopg-amg); [anwalt.de](https://www.anwalt.de/rechtstipps/ist-stoffen-legal-welche-strafen-drohen-bei-steroiden-und-sarms-226362.html)
- **Health: primary sources.** A 2024 analysis of suspected SARM adverse-event cases covers drug-induced liver injury (DILI). A 2024 Cureus case report describes DILI from LGD-4033 (ligandrol). — [PMC10847181](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10847181/); [Cureus 2024 case (PMC11485217)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11485217/)
- **Health: further risks** [secondary]:
  - Ligandrol and RAD-140 are the SARMs most often implicated in liver injury, ranging from mild enzyme rises to jaundice, cholestasis and acute liver failure. In one RAD-140 case, liver enzymes rose above 50 × the upper limit of normal.
  - Suppression of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, which can be marked and prolonged: low testosterone, reduced fertility and libido.
  - HDL falls and LDL rises.
  - Products are often mislabelled or adulterated.
  - The FDA has warned of heart-attack, stroke and liver-damage risk.

- AIS Group D lists SARMs (andarine, ostarine, ligandrol) and prohormones. — [AIS Group D](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_d)

#### Fat burners (synephrine, yohimbine)
- **BfR opinion (2012/2013):**
  - Sports and weight-loss products that combine synephrine (bitter orange) with caffeine may not comply with the law and could be classified as unsafe.

- **BfR safe maximum for synephrine** from supplements: 6.7 mg/day. A Portuguese market study found ~65 % of such supplements exceeded it. [secondary, via that study] — [Revista de Nutrição (SciELO)](https://www.scielo.br/j/rn/a/MvHW4TnRmrPLJzgN8cLGPzD/?format=html&lang=en)

- **Yohimbe in the EU:**
  - Ephedra herb, yohimbe bark and all their preparations are in Part A (prohibited) of the EU list of restricted substances in foods. The year of the yohimbe amendment was not captured. [secondary] — [Food Compliance International](https://foodcomplianceinternational.com/expertise/european-union/food-supplements); [analyze & realize](https://www.a-r.com/the-annex-of-substances-that-are-prohibited-restricted-or-under-scrutiny-is-filling-up-egcg-will-be-added-next-more-to-follow/)
  - EFSA's 2013 assessment was reported as a "non-opinion" that left yohimbe "dead in the water" in Europe. [secondary, headline only] — [NutraIngredients 2013](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2013/07/26/EFSA-non-opinion-leaves-yohimbe-dead-in-the-water-in-Europe/); [EU Commission scientific assessment of yohimbe](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/6b944b04-d315-4469-9984-7669b038ba7a_en?filename=labelling_nutrition-vitamins_minerals-sa_yohimbe_en.pdf)

#### High-dose antioxidants (vitamins C and E)
- **Double-blind RCT** (Nutrition, 2022). In young trained men, vitamins C/E appeared to blunt upper-body strength and hypertrophy gains from RT, while possibly limiting gains in visceral fat. [check: attribution to this RCT via snippet] — [PubMed 36283241](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36283241/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0899900722002611)
- **Clifford et al. 2020** (systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs):
  - Vitamin E alone or with vitamin C "neither enhances nor blunts" training adaptations in muscle strength or lean mass.
  - There is "convincing evidence" that vitamins C and/or E blunt some adaptations to *endurance* training.

  — [PubMed 31851538](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31851538/)
- **Older women with sarcopenia** (Medicine, 2025). Vitamins C+E with 12 weeks of RT gave *better* gains in muscle mass and strength than RT plus placebo. So the effect seems to depend on age and training status. — [Medicine 2025](https://journals.lww.com/md-journal/fulltext/2025/08220/effects_of_vitamins_c_and_e_supplementation.52.aspx)
- **Proposed mechanism:** antioxidants may switch off redox-sensitive signalling in exercised muscle fibres. — [IJERPH 2020 review](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/22/8452)
- **Also exist, results not captured:** Dutra et al. 2020 (meta-analysis of strength training plus C/E) and a 2014 BMC trial report. — [Dutra 2020 (J Sports Med)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2020/3505209); [BMC SSMR 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2052-1847-6-28)

### Inferences
- **Remove from the stack:**
  - BCAAs/EAAs: redundant once the protein target is met; at most a convenience for training fasted.
  - Glutamine.
  - All "T-boosters": tribulus, D-aspartic acid, fenugreek, boron, and ZMA-type zinc/magnesium products unless a deficiency is proven. None has credible hypertrophy data in trained men with normal testosterone, and AIS Group D status signals real contamination risk.
- **Turkesterone/ecdysterone:** the two 2024/2025 human RCTs showed no benefit. WADA monitoring status matters for tested athletes. Product purity is also doubtful, though not verified here.
- **SARMs/prohormones:** legally and medically unacceptable. Risks are liver injury, hormonal suppression and lipid/cardiovascular effects, plus criminal exposure in Germany. The exact threshold rules need legal verification.
- **Fat burners:** avoid synephrine-plus-caffeine products and anything containing yohimbe or yohimbine. Taking them on top of pre-workout caffeine multiplies the cardiovascular load.
- **Antioxidants:** avoid mega-doses of vitamin C (~1 g range) and vitamin E around training. A normal diet, or a standard-dose multivitamin where needed, is a different matter. (Inference; doses not captured.)

### Gaps
- **Tongkat ali and ashwagandha:** the primary meta-analysis data for tongkat ali (effect size, population, safety) and ashwagandha safety warnings (e.g., BfR or Danish liver-injury warnings) were not captured.
- **Zinc, magnesium, boron:** no specific source on testosterone in people without deficiency, beyond the review's statement on boron.

## Q5: Cutting-phase specifics (protein, caffeine, fiber, what helps preserve muscle)

### Takeaway
In a cut:
- **Raise protein** to 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass, higher the leaner you are and the bigger the deficit (Helms 2014).
- **Keep the deficit moderate.** In a meta-regression, a deficit of ~500 kcal/day already blocked lean-mass gains, while strength gains held (Murphy & Koehler 2022).
- **Keep creatine and training volume.**
- **Use caffeine mainly as a training aid,** within EFSA limits and the sleep windows.
- **Avoid stimulant fat burners.**

Evidence on fiber supplements was not captured.

### Cited Findings
- **Helms et al. 2014** (IJSNEM 24(2); systematic review of energy-restricted adults with more than 6 months of RT experience and lower body fat) [older, still the main source]. Protein needs are "likely 2.3–3.1 g/kg of FFM, scaled upwards with severity of caloric restriction and leanness." — [Helms 2014 (IJSNEM)](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/24/2/article-p127.xml); [PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/A%20Systematic%20Review%20of%20Dietary%20Protein%20During%20Caloric%20Restriction%20in%20Resistance%20Trained%20Lean%20Athletes%20-%20A%20Case%20for%20Higher%20Intakes.pdf)
- **Updated review.** A systematic review with meta-regression, "Effect of Dietary Protein on Fat-Free Mass in Energy Restricted, Resistance-Trained Individuals," appeared in the Strength & Conditioning Journal (ahead of print). Its numeric conclusions were not captured. — [SCJ update](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-scj/fulltext/9900/effect_of_dietary_protein_on_fat_free_mass_in.179.aspx)
- **Murphy & Koehler 2022** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 32(1):125–137; meta-analysis and meta-regression):
  - RT in an energy deficit impaired lean-mass gains compared with RT without a deficit (ES −0.57, p = 0.02).
  - Strength gains were comparable (ES −0.31, p = 0.28).
  - A deficit of ~500 kcal/day prevented lean-mass gains.
  - Recommendation: to preserve lean mass during weight loss, avoid deficits above 500 kcal/day.

  — [Murphy & Koehler 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sms.14075); [TUM repository PDF](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/doc/1632530/1632530.pdf)
- **BCAAs:** no added benefit once protein is at least 1.6 g/kg/day, and cutting-phase intakes are higher still (see Q4). — [PMC12655760](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12655760/)
- **Caffeine:** the EFSA 2015 safety limits and the Gardiner 2023 sleep cut-offs apply unchanged (see Q3). — [EFSA](https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/4102); [Gardiner 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1087079223000205)
- **Stimulant fat burners:** BfR warns about synephrine plus caffeine, and yohimbe is prohibited in EU foods (see Q4). — [BfR](https://www.bfr.bund.de/en/opinions/health-assessment-of-sports-and-weight-loss-products-containing-synephrine-and-caffeine/)

### Inferences
- **Protein example:** at 80 kg and ~15 % body fat (FFM 68 kg), 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM = ~155–210 g/day. That is ~2.0–2.6 g/kg of total body weight (arithmetic).
- **Deficit:** at most ~500 kcal/day. Expect to maintain strength rather than gain lean mass while cutting.
- **Creatine:** keep taking it through the cut. Stopping removes the creatine-related water in muscle, which can look like "muscle loss" on the scale or a bioimpedance reading (inference). No creatine data specific to a deficit was captured.
- **Caffeine:** may help sessions on low energy. But lost sleep hurts both appetite regulation and recovery (inference), so stay within 400 mg/day and 200 mg per dose, and respect the timing windows.
- **Fiber:** food-based fiber for satiety. Evidence for glucomannan or psyllium supplements was not captured (see Gaps).
- **Stack in a cut:** the core (protein + creatine) is unchanged, with more protein and possibly less caffeine for evening sessions. Skip the "cutting" category of products (fat burners, BCAAs).

### Gaps
- **Helms update:** numeric conclusions of the SCJ meta-regression update were not captured.
- **Fiber:** the EFSA-authorised glucomannan claim (commonly cited as 3 g/day within an energy-restricted diet) and evidence on fiber/psyllium were not verified.
- **Creatine and caffeine in a deficit:** no creatine data specific to energy deficit was captured, and neither was the size of caffeine's thermogenic or fat-loss effect.
- **Out of scope:** evidence on diet breaks and refeeds (not searched).

## Q6: Evidence classification by authoritative bodies (ISSN, IOC 2018, AIS ABCD, 2024–2026 updates) and the German context

### Takeaway
The main frameworks agree on a short list of evidence-backed performance supplements.
- **IOC 2018 consensus:** caffeine, creatine monohydrate, nitrate, sodium bicarbonate and "possibly" β-alanine have enough evidence for marginal gains.
- **AIS Group A:** caffeine, β-alanine, bicarbonate, beetroot juice/nitrate, creatine and glycerol.
- **ISSN position stands:** caffeine (2021), bicarbonate (2021) and β-alanine (2015) give doses.
- **AIS Group C:** includes HMB and BCAAs.
- **AIS Group D:** includes T-boosters, prohormones, SARMs, stimulants and colostrum.

No details of a 2024–2026 AIS revision were captured, but the framework is still the reference in 2025 studies and policies. The WADA 2026 List and Monitoring Program are current.

For Germany, the NADA-linked Kölner Liste reduces, but does not remove, contamination risk. An IOC-funded study from Cologne found anabolic steroids in ~15 % of supplements internationally and ~11 % in Germany.

### Cited Findings
- **IOC consensus statement 2018** (Maughan et al.; BJSM April 2018, also IJSNEM 28(2)):
  - Caffeine, creatine (monohydrate), nitrate, sodium bicarbonate "and possibly also β-alanine" have adequate support for "marginal performance gains."
  - Performance supplements should be considered only where strong evidence shows them to be safe, legal and effective, and ideally only after the athlete's diet is adequate.
  - Some supplements can harm health, performance, livelihood and reputation, e.g., through an anti-doping rule violation.

  — [IOC 2018 PDF (olympics.com)](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/2018_dietary-supplements-high-performance-athlete.pdf); [PMC5867441](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867441/); [IJSNEM](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/28/2/article-p104.xml)
- **AIS ABCD system.** It ranks sports foods and supplement ingredients by evidence, safety and permitted status:
  - **Group A**, strong evidence for use in sport: caffeine, β-alanine, bicarbonate, beetroot juice, creatine, glycerol.
  - **Group B**, emerging support that needs more research.
  - **Group C**, no proven benefit or not enough research: e.g., magnesium, alpha-lipoic acid, HMB, BCAA/leucine, phosphate, prebiotics, vitamin E, tyrosine.
  - **Group D**, banned or at high risk of contamination: stimulants; prohormones and "hormone boosters" (DHEA, androstenedione, 19-norandrostenedione/-ol); GH releasers and peptides; beta-2 agonists (including higenamine); SARMs (andarine, ostarine, ligandrol); metabolic modulators; colostrum; *Tribulus terrestris* and other T-boosters; maca root powder.

  — [AIS Supplements](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements); [AIS Group C](https://www.ais.gov.au/nutrition/supplements/group_c); [AIS Group D](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_d); [Dietitian Approved summary](https://www.dietitianapproved.com/blog/aissupplementframework) [secondary]
- **Currency of the AIS framework.** The AIS Supplements Framework Committee says it has revised the framework, but no revision date or change list was captured. Two 2025 documents still use the ABCD system: Athletics Australia's supplement policy (May 2025) and a 2025 JISSN survey on athletes' compliance with the AIS classification. — [AIS Supplements](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements); [Athletics Australia policy 2025](https://www.athletics.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/POLICY-AA-Supplements-in-Sport.pdf); [JISSN 2025 survey](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15502783.2025.2496450)
- **ISSN position stands captured:**
  - Caffeine 2021: 3–6 mg/kg, ~60 min before exercise. — [ISSN caffeine](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4)
  - Sodium bicarbonate 2021: 0.2–0.5 g/kg. — [ISSN bicarbonate](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/s12970-021-00458-w)
  - β-alanine 2015: 4–6 g/day for 4 weeks. — [ISSN β-alanine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4501114/)
- **WADA 2026.** The Prohibited List and Monitoring Program have been in force since 1 Jan 2026. Ecdysterone is monitored, not prohibited. — [WADA](https://www.wada-ama.org/en/news/wadas-2026-prohibited-list-now-force); [2026 Monitoring Program PDF](https://www.wada-ama.org/sites/default/files/2025-09/2026_list_monitoring_program_en_final_clean_september_2025.pdf)
- **Germany: Kölner Liste and NADA:**
  - The Kölner Liste is part of the German doping-prevention network and cooperates with NADA. The NADA app now includes it. — [Wikipedia: Kölner Liste](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6lner_Liste_(Doping)); [NADA news](https://www.nada.de/en/service/news/news-detail/nada-app-jetzt-auch-mit-koelner-liste)
  - An IOC-funded international study by the Institute of Biochemistry at the German Sport University Cologne found anabolic steroids in ~15 % of supplements bought in 13 countries, and in ~11 % in Germany. [older; study year not captured] — [Wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6lner_Liste_(Doping)); [Kölner Liste: Hintergrund](https://www.koelnerliste.com/hintergrund/)
  - A Kölner Liste entry is not a recommendation to take supplements. NADA generally advises against regular supplement use without an indication and professional advice. — [Kölner Liste FAQs](https://www.koelnerliste.com/faqs)
  - No positive doping case has been caused by a listed product since 2006. But the Kölner Liste and NADA stress that listing only minimises contamination risk; it cannot exclude it. — [Kölner Liste: Für Sportler*innen](https://www.koelnerliste.com/fuer-sportler-innen); [Kölner Liste product database](https://www.koelnerliste.com/produkt-datenbank)

### Inferences
Summary grading for the described trainee (5 days/week, upper/lower and push/pull, alternating cut and bulk). Every fact is sourced in Q1–Q5; the grades themselves are my synthesis.

| Supplement | Framework status | Evidence for hypertrophy/strength | Dose and timing (from the evidence above) | Main safety notes |

- **Rational stack (inference):**
  - Core, both phases: meet the daily protein target, and take 3–5 g/day creatine monohydrate.
  - Optional: caffeine for key sessions, kept within EFSA and sleep limits.
  - Low-priority extras for high-volume blocks: β-alanine or nitrate.
  - Skip everything else.
- **If the trainee ever competes in a drug-tested federation:** use only Kölner-Liste-listed products, and avoid anything in AIS Group D or on the WADA Monitoring Program.
